Getting started

  1. Install. Run MeshLens-x.y.z-setup.exe from the project's GitHub Releases page. It installs to Program Files, can start MeshLens when you sign in, and can allow the dashboard through the Windows firewall on private (home) networks only. MeshLens then runs in the system tray: right-click the icon for the menu. The icon is green when all is well, amber or red when there are issues.
  2. Dashboard password. Created the first time you open MeshLens. Change it under Settings → Dashboard login.
  3. Router. Settings → ASUS router: the address, SSH port and username are filled in. Type the router's password once. MeshLens keeps it in the Windows Credential Manager and only runs read-only commands.
  4. Wait a few minutes. The router is read every minute (every 15 minutes for settings and device names). Issues and health scores appear after a few polls; trends after two days.
  5. Name your devices. Open a device (Devices tab) and fill in its name, owner and room. Tick Watch for the ones that matter: they also get their own ping monitor.
  6. Optional services. Pi-hole, Home Assistant, UniFi and switch logins can be stored and tested now. The collectors that use them are still to come.

Health scores

Each score runs from 0 to 100: 80+ good, 60–79 fair, below 60 poor.

Device
Starts at 100. Points come off for being kicked (10 each), roam attempts (2 each), disconnects (4 each), a typical signal weaker than -65 dBm (2 per dB) and a slow link (10). Wired devices have no Wi-Fi score.
Mesh unit
Starts at 100. Points come off for radio errors, for many devices dropping off it at once, and for how many of its devices are poor. A unit MeshLens cannot reach scores 0. A unit that is not a managed node has no score.
Network
40% from the average unit score and 60% from the average device score, minus 5 for each high-severity issue (25 at most).

Issues: what they mean

Every issue has three parts. Seen is the evidence MeshLens found. Why is the most likely cause. Try lists fixes, simplest first. Severity: High hurts people now, Medium is worth fixing, Low is minor, Info is just for you to know. You can change the thresholds under Settings → Analysis thresholds.

Kicked off Wi-Fi repeatedly (kick loop)

ASUS's Roaming assistant disconnects a device when its signal falls below a threshold (often -70 dBm). The idea is that it then joins a closer unit. If no closer unit is any better, it rejoins the same one and is kicked again. Each kick costs a few seconds offline, which feels like lag spikes or game disconnects. This is the most common cause of "the Wi-Fi is glitchy" on a mesh.

Fixes: improve that device's signal (move it, move a unit, use 5 GHz at short range, or a cable or powerline adapter). Or lower the threshold to about -75 dBm in the router (Wireless → Professional → Roaming assistant) so it only kicks when the signal is really poor.

USB Wi-Fi adapters (ALFA, TP-Link and others) are often the worst affected. A short USB extension cable moves the adapter away from the PC case and from USB 3 noise. The maker's latest driver helps too. In Device Manager → the adapter → Advanced, set Roaming aggressiveness to low and turn off power saving.

Bounced between units (roam storm)

The device sits where two units look equally good, or equally bad. The mesh keeps trying to move it, and each attempt can stall traffic briefly. Move the device or one unit so that one unit is clearly stronger.

Sticks to a weaker unit

The router found a unit at least 8 dB stronger, but the device stayed where it was. Devices decide for themselves when to roam, and some ignore the mesh's suggestions. Turn Wi-Fi off and on after moving rooms, update the driver, or on Windows raise Roaming aggressiveness one step.

Keeps disconnecting (flapping)

Many disconnects in a short time. If its signal is good, the device itself is leaving: usually power saving, a driver bug, or the device sleeping. If its signal is weak, treat it as a coverage problem.

Weak signal

Signal strength is in dBm, and closer to 0 is stronger: -50 is excellent, -60 good, -70 usable, -80 poor. Below about -70 dBm the link slows down and retries a lot. Every wall, floor, mirror, radiator and fish tank in between makes it weaker.

Slow link

The link rate is the speed the radio negotiated, not your internet speed. It drops with a weak signal or an old Wi-Fi standard. Idle phones and tablets also report low rates to save power, so check again while the device is in use.

Radio errors (failed transmissions)

Each radio counts frames it had to resend and frames that arrived corrupted. These are counted since the unit last restarted. Above about 10% means real interference or struggling devices. 2.4 GHz is the usual culprit, because it is shared with neighbours, microwaves, baby monitors, Bluetooth and USB 3 noise. Use 20 MHz width and channel 1, 6 or 11, and move devices that can use 5 GHz onto it.

Running hot

Give the unit space and airflow. Units in cupboards or in direct sun throttle and become unstable.

Cannot read a unit

MeshLens reads each node over SSH at the address the router lists for it. "Not in the router's mesh list" means the router reports clients on that unit but doesn't list it as a managed AiMesh node. Its clients still appear through the router. Otherwise the unit is off, its backhaul is down, or SSH has not reached it yet (rebooting the node after enabling SSH helps).

Many devices dropped at once

When several devices leave one unit within a minute, the cause is the unit, not the devices. It may be a radio restart, a lost backhaul, a power blip, or a burst of interference. If it repeats, check firmware, the power supply and the backhaul (a cable backhaul rules that out).

Crowded 2.4 GHz radio

All devices on a radio share its airtime. Many slow smart-home devices leave less for everyone else. Move dual-band devices to 5 GHz.

One maker's devices unstable together

When most devices from one maker misbehave, suspect their Wi-Fi chip or driver rather than where they are. Update drivers or firmware and turn off power saving.

Aggressive roaming threshold

A Roaming assistant threshold above -70 dBm kicks devices whose signal is still usable. About -70 to -75 dBm suits most homes.

WPS on

WPS is a known weak point and rarely needed. Turn it off unless you use it.

Router admin reachable from the internet

Turn off web access from WAN, or limit it to known addresses.

Device in the DMZ

All incoming internet connections go to that device. Use specific port forwards instead.

UPnP on

Devices can open ports on the router by themselves. This suits consoles and video calls, but it is a risk if a device is compromised.

IGMP snooping off

Multicast traffic (casting, IPTV, Sonos, smart-home discovery) then goes to every device. On Wi-Fi that uses airtime at the slowest rate. If you use IPTV or cast a lot, try turning it on and check that casting still works.

Watched device not seen

A device you marked as watched has been off the network longer than the limit in Analysis thresholds.

New device

A device joined for the first time. Phones with private (randomised) addresses can look new when they change address. Name the ones you know.

Slow powerline link

devolo adapters report the radio (PHY) rate between each pair; usable throughput is roughly half. Below about 50 Mbit/s a link struggles with video calls plus anything else; below 20 it is barely usable. Powerline runs over the house wiring, so it is slowed by distance, different circuits, extension leads and surge protectors, and by electrical noise (phone and laptop chargers, LED drivers, fridges, washing machines). Plug adapters straight into wall sockets and try other sockets. A link that is fast one way and very slow the other (for example 182 and 7 Mbit/s) usually means noise near one adapter.

Lag between this PC and the router

Pings that never leave the house are slow or lost, so the problem is in the home network, not the internet. If the MeshLens PC itself is on Wi-Fi, its own link is included; a cable to the router makes every other measurement more trustworthy.

Internet drops or delays packets

Pings to internet targets lose packets or spike while pings to the router stay clean at the same time. That points at the broadband line or the ISP. Note the times (the Tests tab keeps a month) and check they don't line up with heavy use at home (see bufferbloat). If they don't, they are good evidence for the ISP.

Pings to a mesh unit are lost

The unit or its backhaul is struggling. Check its backhaul on the Topology tab.

A watched device misses pings

Often power saving (phones, consoles in rest mode). If it happens while the device is in use, line it up with its Wi-Fi journey.

Bufferbloat

Latency while the line is full, compared with idle. When someone uploads (cloud backup, photo sync) or downloads, packets queue in the router or modem and games and calls lag even though "speed" looks fine. Grades: A+ under 5 ms extra, A under 30, B under 60, C under 200, D under 400, F above. The fix is the router's QoS: on ASUS, Adaptive QoS → QoS → enable, with the bandwidth set to about 90% of your measured speeds.

Slower than your plan

Set your plan's speeds under Settings → Monitors and speed tests. A test from a PC on Wi-Fi measures the Wi-Fi too; test on a cable before calling the ISP.

Speed test failed

The test server could not be reached, or the connection dropped during the test.

A service worth checking

The port scan found a service that is a common way in on gadgets with default settings (Telnet, FTP, open camera streams, Android debugging, databases without passwords). It is fine where you expect it and it has a password; otherwise turn it off. MeshLens only scans inside your home network.

New open port

A device started offering a service it didn't before: an update, a setting or new software.

Using MeshLens on your phone

Press 📱 Phone at the top of the dashboard on the PC and point your phone's camera at the QR code. The phone must be on the home Wi-Fi (not mobile data or the guest network). The dialog also lists every address that works:

  • The PC's name in the router's DNS (for example http://minisf.yourdomain:8787): works from any device that uses the router or Pi-hole for DNS, and doesn't change. This is the one in the QR code when the router knows the name.
  • The PC's address: always works, but can change; reserve it in the router to keep it.
  • http://meshlens.local:8787: MeshLens announces this name itself (mDNS). iPhones, iPads, Macs and Windows understand it; many Android browsers don't.

To make it feel like an app: on iPhone open it in Safari → Share → Add to Home Screen; on Android open it in Chrome → ⋮ → Add to Home screen. Phones stay logged in for 30 days. If a phone can't connect at all, check the installer's firewall option (or allow MeshLens on private networks in Windows Security).

Graphs

The Graphs tab draws everything MeshLens records for a chosen period (6 hours to 30 days). Hover any chart for exact values; click a legend entry to hide that line.

Wi-Fi devices online
How many devices were on each unit and each band. A unit that suddenly drops to zero has restarted or lost its backhaul.
Wi-Fi events
Kicks, roam attempts, disconnects and refused joins per interval. Spikes line up with complaints.
Internet traffic
Download and upload through the router's internet connection, averaged per router poll. Compare with lag: a full upload is the classic cause of bufferbloat.
Wi-Fi traffic per unit and band
Data through each radio. Shows which unit and band carry the load.
Latency and packet loss
Every monitored target on one chart; loss for the router and internet targets.
When is the internet laggy?
A week of internet latency by day and hour. A colour pattern at the same hours every day points at congestion at home (evening streaming, backups) or at the ISP.
Signal strength
How many connected devices sit in each signal band right now.
Radio transmit failures
Each radio's failure rate over time; a rise that comes and goes is interference.
Powerline link speeds
The slower direction of each adapter pair over time.

Every device has its own graphs too (click it anywhere): signal, which unit and band it was on (each colour is a unit/band, so roams and kicks show as colour changes), link speed, data used, Wi-Fi events, ping latency (for watched devices) and its daily health score.

Router tab

Everything MeshLens reads from each unit, in one place (read-only):

  • Identity and health: model, firmware, address, how long it has been up (a short uptime means it restarted), CPU load, and how it connects to the rest of the mesh (backhaul band and signal).
  • Radios and networks: every Wi-Fi interface with its network name, band, channel, width, transmit power, devices, traffic now, and the radio's failure and error rates and temperature. Hidden mesh backhaul networks are greyed.
  • How busy its channels are: the access point's own measurement, heard by this PC (see below).
  • Ports: each Ethernet port, whether something is plugged in and at what speed. Amber means slower than the port can do, for example a 2.5G port linked at 1G because the other end or the cable is 1G.
  • Devices on the unit now (click one for details), radio and network settings explained in plain English, and every raw value MeshLens reads. Change settings in the ASUS app or web UI; MeshLens never does.

System: processor, memory and more

Every minute MeshLens reads each unit's processor time, memory, chip temperatures, connection table and storage (standard read-only Linux files). CPU busy is the share of time the processor was working; busiest core matters because network packet work often lands on one core, so one maxed-out core can slow traffic while the average looks fine. Memory used counts what the system can't free. Connections tracked is the router's table of active connections (NAT); when it is full, new connections fail. Restarts are spotted when a unit's uptime goes back to zero.

Processor very busy

The unit's CPU (or one core) is busy most of the time. Restart it if it has been up for weeks, update the firmware, and turn off router features you don't use (Traffic Analyzer, AiProtection).

Low on memory

Most memory is in use; the router may drop connections or restart services. Restart; if it fills again within days, update the firmware.

Chip hot

The hottest temperature sensor is high. Give the unit space and airflow.

Connection table nearly full

New connections will fail. A device opening huge numbers of connections (file sharing, a misbehaving camera) is the usual cause.

Storage full

Logs or settings can't be written. A restart clears temporary storage.

Unit restarted

Its uptime went back to zero. Unplanned restarts point at power, heat, memory or firmware.

Firmware update available

The router itself reports a newer firmware. Update from the ASUS app or web UI.

QoS off while bufferbloat is high

Turn on Adaptive QoS with the bandwidth set to about 90% of the router speed test's results; that keeps games and calls responsive while someone uploads or downloads.

This PC as a network sensor

MeshLens uses the PC it runs on to measure what the routers don't report:

Air survey
Every 2 minutes the PC's Wi-Fi card lists every access point it can hear (it does this even when not connected). ASUS units, like most modern access points, include their own measurement of how much of the time their channel is busy ("BSS Load", channel utilisation) and how many devices they have. Above about 50% devices queue to transmit; above 75% it feels slow and laggy. Neighbours' networks on your channels are shown too.
Broadcast and multicast
Every minute MeshLens reads the wired adapter's counters. Every device must handle broadcast and multicast traffic and on Wi-Fi it is sent at the slowest rate, so a flood (for example from a network scanner box) slows everything. A quiet home network is under about 30 broadcast packets a second.
Wi-Fi link
When the PC's own Wi-Fi is connected: network, band, channel, signal and link speed.

It can only hear what reaches the PC: put it (or later an ESP32 probe) where you want to measure.

Lots of broadcast and multicast traffic

The PC receives far more broadcast/multicast packets than a home network normally carries. Find the source by powering devices off one at a time while watching Graphs → Broadcast and multicast; network scanners and looped cables are the usual causes. Turning on IGMP snooping keeps multicast to the devices that want it.

A channel is very busy

The access point itself reports its channel in use most of the time. On 2.4 GHz move everything that can use 5 GHz off it, keep 20 MHz width and pick the quietest of channels 1, 6 and 11. Slow, distant devices use the most airtime.

Neighbours on your channel

Several strong neighbouring networks share the channel and take turns with yours. A different channel may be quieter.

The MeshLens PC gets much less than the line

The router's own speed test measures the internet line; the PC's test also includes its path to the router (Wi-Fi, powerline, switches). When the PC gets far less, that path is the limit, and every device using the same path is limited too.

Topology map

The Diagram view draws the network: the internet (with the last speed test, bufferbloat grade and latency), the router and each mesh unit (health score, devices per band, worst radio error rate), powerline adapters, and every device as a dot (colour = signal; red ring = poor health; blue ring = watched; click for details). Links show how units connect: Wi-Fi backhaul with band and signal for each radio (dotted, coloured by signal), powerline with its speed each way (dashed), or Ethernet. Save PNG / Save SVG download the picture. The List view shows the same as a tree of names.

What is connected to what, built only from things MeshLens has actually measured:

  • Mesh units sit under the unit they connect through. A Wi-Fi backhaul shows its band, signal and rate (the node's backhaul radio appears as a client of its parent). A node bridged behind a powerline adapter shows as wired through it.
  • Powerline adapters (⚡): the one wired to the router hangs off the router; the others hang off it with the measured rates each way.
  • Devices sit under the unit or adapter they use, with band and signal for Wi-Fi. Click any device for its details. Devices offline for 15 minutes are left out.

Switches without a management interface can't be seen directly; wired devices behind them appear under the unit or adapter the switch is plugged into.

Powerline (devolo)

MeshLens reads the devolo Magic adapters through the same device API the devolo app and Home Assistant use, read-only, no password needed. Any one adapter reports the whole powerline network: every adapter, which is wired to the router, the devices behind each one, and the rate between every pair. Rates are kept for a month so slow spells show up.

Latency, jitter and loss

MeshLens pings the router, each mesh unit, internet targets (1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8) and your watched devices every 2 seconds, and keeps one summary per minute for a month.

Latency
Round-trip time. On a cable the router is under 1 ms, on good Wi-Fi a few ms. Internet targets are usually 5 to 30 ms.
Worst (p95)
95% of pings were faster than this. It shows spikes that an average hides.
Jitter
How much latency changes from one ping to the next. Calls and games care about it more than latency itself; under 10 ms is good.
Loss
Pings with no reply. Any steady loss to the router means a home problem; loss only to internet targets points at the ISP.

Comparing targets tells you where a problem is: router bad means home network; router fine but internet bad means the line or the ISP. The optional ISP first hop target measures the first router on the ISP's side the way tracert does. Routers answer those probes at low priority, so it is off by default and never used to raise issues on its own.

Speed test and bufferbloat

Two places to test from. On the router uses the speed test built into the ASUS firmware (Ookla), so it measures the internet line itself with nothing in the way. It is off until you switch it on under Settings → Monitors and speed tests, because running it accepts Ookla's licence and privacy terms. Results of tests you run in the ASUS app or web UI are read and shown anyway. While it runs, MeshLens pings from the router to measure bufferbloat. From this PC measures the line plus the PC's own path to the router. Compare the two: a big gap means the path, not the line, is slow. ESP32 probes will add tests from wherever you place them.

Three phases, about 25 seconds: idle latency, then download with 4 parallel streams while pinging, then upload while pinging. By default it uses Cloudflare's speed test servers: no account, and nothing about your network is sent, only the test traffic. Each test moves a few hundred MB. Scheduled tests run every 6 hours (change it, or turn it off, under Settings → Monitors and speed tests); the latency monitor pauses during a test. The result reflects the MeshLens PC's own connection too.

Discovery and ports

MeshLens finds devices on your subnets every 15 minutes with a paced ping sweep (at most 200 probes a second) and the PC's ARP table, which also catches devices that ignore ping. It learns names from reverse DNS, NetBIOS (Windows PCs, NAS), mDNS/Bonjour (Apple, Google Cast, printers, smart home) and UPnP (TVs, consoles, media servers), and shows which source each name came from. Once a day it checks about 85 common ports on each device seen that day with ordinary connection attempts, reads web page titles and banners, and records when ports open or close. Type is a best guess from maker, services and ports; your own type always wins. Nothing outside your subnets is scanned.

Mesh units

The router and its AiMesh nodes. MeshLens finds the nodes from the router's own list and reads each one. Chip shows the radio family (MediaTek on the BT8, Qualcomm on the BD4), because they report things differently.

Radio health

For each radio: transmit failures (frames that needed resending), receive errors (corrupted frames), temperature and background noise. Noise closer to 0 is worse; -95 dBm is quiet.

Least stable devices

Devices ranked by kicks, roam attempts and disconnects in the last 24 hours. The weakest signal is the lowest the router reported in its roaming checks.

Trends and week on week

Every hour MeshLens writes a daily summary for each device: kicks, roam attempts, connects, disconnects, signal and a health score. Summaries are kept for 12 months; detailed data for one month. Week on week compares the last 7 days with the 7 before. Use it after changing something, such as moving a unit or lowering the roaming threshold, to see whether it helped.

Devices

Everything the router knows about, wired and Wi-Fi. Private MAC means the device uses a randomised address (most phones do), so the maker cannot be looked up. Makers are looked up in a list stored on this PC; nothing is sent to the internet.

Wi-Fi events

Connected / Disconnected
The device joined or left a unit.
Kicked (weak signal)
The roaming assistant disconnected it (see kick loop).
Roam attempt
The router checked whether another unit would be better, with both signal readings.
Moved to another unit
The device now shows on a different unit, so the old one cleared it.
Left (signal)
A Qualcomm (BD4) unit recorded the device leaving, with its last signal.
Association failed
The device tried to join and was refused, for example with a wrong password or too many devices.

Device journey

One device's day in order: where it joined, when it was kicked or moved, and how long it was offline in between. Click any device name to see it.

Your notes

A name, owner, room, type and tags that you set. Your values always win over what MeshLens detects and are never overwritten.

Settings: dashboard login

One password protects the dashboard for every phone and laptop on your network. After 5 wrong tries the dashboard waits 5 minutes. Changing the password logs out every other browser. Forgotten it? On the MeshLens PC, delete web_auth.json in %LOCALAPPDATA%\MeshLens and restart MeshLens.

Settings: network

The subnets that make up your home network (for example 10.0.10.0/23 covers 10.0.10.0 to 10.0.11.255). Device discovery and port checks (still to come) only look inside these. Add addresses to the exclude list to leave them alone.

Settings: ASUS router

SSH must be enabled on the router (Administration → System → Enable SSH, LAN only). MeshLens logs in and runs only read-only commands. These are listed in its source in commands.py, and it never changes a setting. The password is tied to the address, so moving the router to a new address asks for it again.

Settings: Pi-hole

For Pi-hole v6, create an app password under Settings → Web interface / API (switch to Expert), and paste it here. For v5, use the API token from Settings → API. Test connection logs in, reads today's query count and logs out.

Settings: Home Assistant

In Home Assistant, open your profile → Security → Long-lived access tokens → Create token. A user that is not an administrator is enough.

Settings: UniFi

Use a local admin with the View Only role and no two-factor login. In the UniFi Network app go to Settings → Admins & Users and add an admin restricted to local access. The local controller on this PC is https://127.0.0.1:8443 and uses a self-signed certificate, so leave certificate checking off for it.

Settings: switches and other devices

Logins for TP-Link Easy Smart switches, devolo adapters and other network gear. MeshLens will use them read-only to show ports, link speeds and errors, and which device is behind which port. Found on your network suggests wired devices whose maker usually makes network equipment. Passwords are kept in the Windows Credential Manager, tied to the address and username. Removing a device also removes its password.

Settings: powerline

On by default. MeshLens finds devolo adapters among the devices it discovers; if it doesn't, enter one adapter's address (any one is enough). Set when a link counts as slow.

Settings: discovery and ports

How often to look for devices, names and services, and to scan ports (0 = only when you press Scan ports). The rate limit caps probes per second so cameras and smart plugs are never flooded. Ports 1-1024 is more thorough but takes longer per device.

Settings: monitors and speed tests

Ping interval and targets (add a game server's address to watch it), whether to include mesh units, watched devices and the ISP's first router, how often to run speed tests, and your plan's speeds so MeshLens can tell you when you get less.

Settings: analysis thresholds

These control when an issue is raised. For example, Kick loop: 3 kicks within 60 minutes. Raise them if MeshLens reports too much, lower them to catch problems sooner.

Settings: data kept

Detailed data (every poll, every event) is kept for 31 days by default, and daily summaries for 366 days. Everything stays on this PC in %LOCALAPPDATA%\MeshLens.

Settings: web interface

0.0.0.0 lets phones and laptops on your network open the dashboard. 127.0.0.1 limits it to this PC. Windows may ask once to allow MeshLens through the firewall; allow it for private networks only.

Updates

MeshLens checks the project's GitHub releases once a day (Settings → Updates; you can turn it off). When a newer version is out, a banner appears on the dashboard and the tray menu shows Install update. Installing downloads the setup file from the release, checks it against the release's published SHA-256 checksum, and runs it; Windows asks once for permission. MeshLens closes, updates and starts again, usually within a minute, and the dashboard reconnects on its own. Settings, history and passwords are kept, and the database is upgraded automatically. You can also always download the latest MeshLens-x.y.z-setup.exe from the releases page and run it over the top of the installed version.

The check only asks GitHub which release is the latest; nothing about your network is sent.

Exporting data

Settings → Export data downloads what MeshLens has stored for a period you choose: Wi-Fi events, per-device signal samples, latency per minute, speed tests, radio health, traffic counters, the air survey, broadcast/multicast counts, powerline rates, inventory changes, daily summaries and the device list with your notes. CSV opens in Excel; JSON suits scripts; several datasets at once come as a ZIP with one CSV each. Times are local; device and unit names are added next to MAC addresses. The files contain your network's device names and addresses, so keep them private.

Troubleshooting

"Authentication failed" for the router
The router password is missing or wrong. Enter it again under Settings → ASUS router.
"Connection refused" or a timeout
SSH is off on the router or on a different port. Check Administration → System on the router.
A node shows "not reachable"
See Cannot read a unit.
No issues and no scores
Wait for a few router polls. Settings → Status shows the last poll and any error.
Phones can't open the dashboard
Check Settings → Web interface listens on 0.0.0.0, and that the Windows firewall allows MeshLens on private networks.
Logs
%LOCALAPPDATA%\MeshLens\logs\meshlens.log. Start with --debug for more detail.

Privacy and safety

  • Read-only everywhere: MeshLens never changes your router, switches or services.
  • Passwords and tokens are kept in the Windows Credential Manager. They are never written to files or logs, and never shown in the dashboard.
  • No device data leaves your network. Maker lookups use a list stored on this PC.

Glossary

dBm (signal)
Signal strength; closer to 0 is stronger. -50 excellent, -60 good, -70 usable, -80 poor.
2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz
The Wi-Fi bands. 2.4 GHz reaches furthest but is slow and crowded. 5 GHz is faster with shorter range. 6 GHz is fastest, shortest range, and needs Wi-Fi 6E or 7 devices.
Roaming
A device moving from one mesh unit to another.
Roaming assistant
ASUS's feature that kicks devices below a signal threshold so they move to a closer unit.
Backhaul
The link between mesh units (wireless or cable).
Link rate
The speed the radio negotiated with the device, in Mbit/s.
Airtime
The share of time a radio is busy sending. Slow devices use more of it for the same data.
Private / randomised MAC
A made-up hardware address that phones use for privacy.
